Is a 401(k) Worth It?

The 401(k) is the most common retirement plan in America — but is it actually a good deal? Here's the honest breakdown.

Short answer: yes, almost always. But the answer depends heavily on whether your employer offers a match and how high the plan fees are.

Pros

  • Employer match = free money. Most companies match 50–100% of your contribution, up to 3–6% of salary. That's an instant 50–100% return.
  • Tax break. Traditional 401(k) contributions reduce your taxable income. $10,000 in contributions saves you $2,200–$3,200 in taxes (depending on your bracket).
  • Automatic. Contributions come out of your paycheck before you see the money. Out of sight, out of mind.
  • Higher limits than IRA. In 2026, you can contribute $24,500 to a 401(k) vs. only $7,500 to an IRA.

Cons

  • High fees. Some 401(k) plans charge 1%+ annually in administrative and fund fees. On $500k, that's $5,000/year — a huge drag.
  • Limited investment choices. You can only pick from the funds your plan offers. Often expensive, poorly performing options.
  • Early withdrawal penalty. Take money out before 59.5 and you pay a 10% penalty plus taxes.
  • Vesting schedule. Employer match may require 3–5 years before you own it fully. Leave early and you lose it.

When a 401(k) Is Clearly Worth It

If your employer offers any match at all, you should contribute at least enough to get the full match. This is non-negotiable — it's the best return you'll ever get on your money.

Example: You earn $75,000. Your employer matches 50% on the first 6% of salary. If you contribute 6% ($4,500/year), your employer adds $2,250/year. That's a 50% instant return — before any investment growth.

When to Skip or Minimize It

A 401(k) might not be worth it if:

No employer match + high fees. If your plan charges 1.5% annually and there's no match, you're better off putting money in a low-cost IRA (which has more investment choices and lower fees). You can always max out the 401(k) later.

You need the money before 59.5. The 10% penalty makes it a bad idea for short-term savings. Use a taxable brokerage account instead.

The Ideal Order of Operations

Financial planners generally agree on this priority:

1. Contribute enough to 401(k) to get the full employer match.
2. Max out your IRA (Roth or Traditional — better investment choices, lower fees).
3. Go back and max out your 401(k) if you still have money to save.
4. Use a taxable brokerage account for anything beyond that.
