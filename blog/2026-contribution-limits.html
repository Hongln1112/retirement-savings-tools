2026 Retirement Contribution Limits: The Complete Guide

Updated October 2026 · 8 min read

Every year, the IRS adjusts retirement account contribution limits for inflation. If you don't know the current limits, you're either leaving tax-advantaged savings on the table or accidentally over-contributing (which triggers penalties). Here's the complete breakdown for 2026.

401(k), 403(b), and 457 Plans

Account TypeEmployee Contribution LimitCatch-Up (50+)
401(k), 403(b), 457$23,500+$7,500
Total (employer + employee)$70,000+$7,500

This is your pre-tax (or Roth) contribution from your salary. Your employer's match doesn't count against your limit — it's part of the total $70,000 cap.

IRA (Traditional and Roth)

Account TypeContribution LimitCatch-Up (50+)
Traditional IRA$7,000+$1,000
Roth IRA$7,000+$1,000

The $7,000 limit applies to combined contributions across all your Traditional and Roth IRAs. You can split it any way you want.

HSA (Health Savings Account)

Coverage TypeContribution LimitCatch-Up (55+)

HSA is the most tax-advantaged account available: triple tax-free (contributions deductible, growth tax-free, qualified medical withdrawals tax-free). After 65, you can withdraw for any penalty-free.

Should You Max Everything?

Not all at once — unless you're a high earner. The optimal order is:

1. Contribute to your 401(k) up to the full employer match (free money).
2. Max out your HSA if you have a high-deductible health plan.
3. Max out your IRA (Roth or Traditional based on your tax situation).
4. Go back and max out your 401(k) the rest of the way.
5. Use a taxable brokerage for anything beyond that.

Contribution limits are based on IRS announcements and estimates. Always verify at irs.gov before making contribution decisions. This guide is for educational purposes only and is not tax or financial advice.
